# M1 — Foundation

**Status:** approved · **Date:** 2026-09-03 · **Milestone:** M1 of M1–M6

The foundation milestone: `packages/contracts`, `packages/money`,
`services/tenancy` and `services/identity` on Postgres with versioned
migrations, tenant context in `packages/service-kit`, RLS policies, and
`docker compose` working end to end.

Approved decisions are recorded here rather than in the ADRs they touch. Where a
decision strengthens or amends an accepted ADR, the amendment is noted inline so
a future reader is not misled by the older text.

## D1 — Money representation

`BIGINT` paise in Postgres. In TypeScript, `Paise` is a branded **`bigint`**,
not a branded number.

> **Amends ADR-0012.** The ADR specified integer paise with enforcement by an
> ESLint restriction. Branding a `bigint` moves enforcement into the type
> system: `bigint * number` fails at compile time *and* throws at runtime, and
> `bigint * bigint` returns an unbranded `bigint`, so the result fails on
> assignment back into a money field. ESLint remains a genuine backstop rather
> than the primary mechanism.

- ESLint backstop: `no-restricted-syntax` on `*` and `/` within
  `services/*/src/domain/**`, with `packages/money` exempt. A CI test proves a
  deliberate violation fails lint — the same shape as M2's cross-service-import
  test.
- **Transport is a digit-only string of paise** (`"1234500"`). JSON has no
  bigint. This is not the round-trip ADR-0012 forbids: what it forbids is
  *formatted* strings (`₹12,345.00`) through `parseFloat`. The canonical string
  is parsed only by `packages/money`, and its Zod schema rejects any separator,
  decimal point or misplaced sign.
- Rates are `BasisPoints` — a branded integer, 1 bp = 0.01%. Covers the everyday
  cases: 194Q 0.1% = 10 bp, 194C-lower 0.75% = 75 bp, GST 18% = 1800 bp.

> **Amended 2026-09-03, during step 2.** Integer basis points are not sufficient.
> Rates are exact rationals — `Ratio { num: bigint, den: bigint }` — and
> `BasisPoints` survives as sugar, with `mulRate` a thin wrapper over
> `mulRatio`. No call site changes.
>
> Three rate families this domain reaches cannot be expressed in whole basis
> points, and each is exact as a ratio:
>
> | Rate | Why bp fails |
> |---|---|
> | Sec 195 / Form 27Q composite: 10% × 1.02 surcharge × 1.04 cess = **10.608%** | 1060.8 bp is not an integer. The 27Q return format's own rate field carries four decimals |
> | Rule 35 CGST — tax from a tax-inclusive value, `rate/(100+rate)` | 18/118 recurring |
> | Per-day interest, Sec 50 CGST and Sec 201(1A) | 18%/365 recurring |
>
> On a ₹50,00,000 fee the composite rate differs from the nearest whole basis
> point by ₹400 — an arithmetic fact. Whether that constitutes a statutory
> short deduction is an M2.5 question for the CA, not a claim made here.
>
> The same change gives division a home. `ratioOf(a, b)` divides money by money
> exactly and returns a dimensionless `Ratio`, so proration — an RA bill's share
> of a contract, Rule 42/43 ITC reversal, advance recovery — loses nothing at
> the point of division. The loss happens once, later, at a named boundary.

### The multiply primitive

```ts
mulRate(amount: Paise, rate: BasisPoints, boundary: RoundingBoundary): Paise
```

`boundary` is **required and has no default**. It is impossible to multiply
money without naming the statutory boundary.

> **Amends ADR-0012.** The ADR asked that rounding boundaries be "named, and
> carry the citation in a comment". A comment is a convention; a required
> parameter is a compiler error. This signature is kept exactly as written.

Each boundary is a separate exported function carrying its citation and its own
golden-file test:

| Function | Statute | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| `roundToPaise` | *none — commercial convention* | nearest paise |
| `roundGstPerInvoicePerHead` | Sec 170, CGST Act | nearest rupee, per invoice, per tax head |
| `roundTdsSec288B` | Sec 288B, IT Act | nearest ₹10 |
| `roundChallanWholeRupees` | challan format (ITNS-281 has no paise column) | whole rupees |

**Amended 2026-09-15 by the CA's written answers** (provisional —
`docs/statutory/QUESTIONS-FOR-CA.md`). CA-02 replaced `roundGstPerInvoicePerHead`
with `roundGstHeadToPaise`, each head kept to the paise, and
`roundInvoiceTotalSec170`, the invoice total to the rupee with its round-off a
line of its own. CA-03 took Sec 288B off each deduction:
`roundTdsDeductionToPaise`. CA-04 replaced `roundChallanWholeRupees` and
`roundTdsSec288B` with `roundChallanSec288B` — paise dropped, then the nearest
₹10. The table above is the plan as it was written.

`roundToPaise` was added during step 2 and is **not** statutory. Every external
format carries paise-precision LINE values with a separate document-level
round-off — the e-invoice IRP schema puts rupee rounding in `RndOffAmt`, GSTR-1
B2B tables are 2-decimal, Tally books line tax in paise against a Round Off
ledger. Without a paise boundary the only rounding available is to the rupee, so
a developer computing a line would either apply Sec 170 per line — wrong, and it
drifts without bound in the line count — or bypass the package entirely.

Separate functions rather than inline constants, so a CA correction in M2.5 is
one function body plus a golden-file regeneration.

## D2 — The GST property test

The original wording — `CGST + SGST === totalGST` — is **replaced, not
supplemented**. It is definitional: it holds trivially wherever `totalGST` is
the sum, and would pass while hiding the bug it exists to catch. A definitional
assertion sitting beside a real one invites someone to delete the wrong one
later.

Asserted under `fast-check`:

1. `cgst === sgst` for every intra-state supply.
2. `cgst + sgst === totalGst` — definitional; guards the accessor only.
3. **`|igst(1800bp) − (cgst(900bp) + sgst(900bp))| ≤ ₹1`** — the
   rounding-placement property. This is the one that fails when someone rounds
   per line instead of per invoice per head.
4. No operation yields a non-integer or a `number`.

The bound in (3) is exact and tight, and — established during step 2 — it is
**independent of the rounding mode**. Writing `R(c) = c + e` with `|e| ≤ ½` for
any nearest-mode rounding, `2·R(c) − R(2c) = 2e₁ − e₂`, so the divergence is at
most 1.5 and, being a whole number of rupees, at most ₹1. Brute-forced over
bases ₹0–₹4000 under half-away-from-zero, half-up, half-even and truncation: all
four give a maximum of exactly ₹1. Only the *golden values* depend on the mode
being half-away-from-zero.

**Property 3 alone does not catch per-line rounding**, which was the defect it
was written for: a single-amount generator makes each amount its own invoice. A
separate multi-line assertion covers it — 40 lines of ₹5.00 at 9% give ₹0 when
rounded per line against ₹18 for the invoice, and the drift grows with the line
count, so no fixed tolerance absorbs it.

## D3 — Pooling and tenant context

Every request runs inside `withTenant(ctx, fn)` in `packages/service-kit`:

```
BEGIN
  SELECT set_config('app.tenant_id', $1, true)   -- true = LOCAL to this tx
  SELECT set_config('app.user_id',   $2, true)
  ...fn...
COMMIT
```

**`set_config(..., true)` rather than literal `SET LOCAL`.** `SET LOCAL` takes
no bind parameters, so the alternative is interpolating a tenant id into SQL
inside the layer whose entire job is tenant isolation.

> **Settles OPEN-DECISIONS "Postgres pooling vs `SET LOCAL`".**
> `_legacy/atelier-current/app/lib/db.js:319` is not a seam to preserve —
> `withTransaction` is a no-op in cloud mode (see STACK-MIGRATION.md). This is
> built new.

Three things make the guarantee real rather than nominal.

### Two database roles

| Role | Owns tables | `BYPASSRLS` | Connects via | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `app_migrator` | yes | no | **Postgres directly, 5432** | migrations, DDL |
| `app_runtime` | no | no | PgBouncer, transaction mode, 6432 | every request |

A table owner bypasses its own policies by default, so every table also gets
`ALTER TABLE … FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY`.

### Migrations bypass the pooler

`app_migrator` connects directly on 5432. Transaction-mode pooling breaks DDL
that needs session state, and drizzle-kit takes advisory locks that do not
survive connection reuse. Two connection strings in compose:

| Variable | Target |
|---|---|
| `DATABASE_URL` | PgBouncer, transaction mode, 6432 |
| `MIGRATION_DATABASE_URL` | Postgres directly, 5432 |

The isolation suite is the only thing that *must* go through the pooler.

### Revised after the isolation review (2026-09-03)

The review found that **two of the five tests measure the wrong state**, and
that the policy shape as sketched is not sufficient. Corrections, all of which
land before the first policy is written:

**The empty-string state is the one production is always in.** On a fresh
connection `current_setting('app.tenant_id', true)` is `NULL`, and
`tenant_id = NULL` denies — correct. But on any connection where a transaction
has *ever* set it LOCAL and ended, the placeholder exists with an **empty
string** value, and `''::uuid` raises `22P02`. So an unwrapped query returns
zero rows in a fresh Testcontainers session and a **500 in production**. Every
policy therefore goes through one function:

```sql
CREATE FUNCTION tenancy.current_tenant_id() RETURNS uuid
  LANGUAGE sql STABLE PARALLEL SAFE
  RETURN NULLIF(current_setting('app.tenant_id', true), '')::uuid;
```

STABLE SQL inlines, so `tenant_id = tenancy.current_tenant_id()` remains a
btree key on `tenant_id`.

**Two policies per table, not one.** Policies default to PERMISSIVE and
permissive policies are OR-ed, so a later `CREATE POLICY debug_all ON t USING
(true)` opens the table while still satisfying "at least one policy present".
RESTRICTIVE policies are AND-ed with everything, but restrictive-only means no
rows are visible — hence the pair, both with explicit `USING` **and**
`WITH CHECK`:

```sql
CREATE POLICY tenant_isolation ON <t> AS RESTRICTIVE FOR ALL
  USING (tenant_id = tenancy.current_tenant_id())
  WITH CHECK (tenant_id = tenancy.current_tenant_id());
CREATE POLICY tenant_access ON <t> AS PERMISSIVE FOR ALL
  USING (tenant_id = tenancy.current_tenant_id())
  WITH CHECK (tenant_id = tenancy.current_tenant_id());
```

Omitting `WITH CHECK` where policies are later split per command is a **write**
attack, not a read one: `INSERT ... tenant_id = <B>` plants a payable in another
tenant's books, and `UPDATE ... SET tenant_id = <B>` pushes a row across. For an
ERP, poisoning is worse than exfiltration.

**`tenant_id` is never `DEFAULT current_tenant_id()`.** It is stamped explicitly
by the repository from `ctx.tenantId`. A forgotten stamp then fails `NOT NULL`,
a wrong one fails RLS, and a bad id from a job fails the FK — instead of
silently creating a ghost row.

**Referential integrity bypasses RLS entirely** — the docs are explicit that
unique/PK/FK checks are exempt. With a single-column FK, tenant A can insert a
row referencing tenant B's project: the insert succeeds, confirming existence,
and `ON DELETE CASCADE` then lets B's delete remove A's row. Unique constraints
leak values through the error `DETAIL: Key (gstin)=(...) already exists`.
Therefore: PK is `(tenant_id, id)` on every tenant-scoped table, every FK is
composite `(tenant_id, x)`, and every unique constraint includes `tenant_id`.
A `tenantTable()` helper is the only sanctioned way to declare a table, and
lint bans bare `pgTable(`.

**`TRUNCATE` is not subject to RLS.** The runtime role is granted
`SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE` and never `TRUNCATE`.

**The `pg` pool, not PgBouncer, is where connection reuse actually bites.**
`pool.connect()` followed by a `release()` on an error path returns a client
still inside tenant A's open transaction; `pg-pool` does not roll back for you.
So `withTenant` is the **only** caller of `pool.connect()`, no `pool` or `db` is
exported, `client.release(true)` is used on every error path, and
`no-floating-promises` is an error — an un-awaited query from A's handler would
otherwise execute inside B's transaction.

**Also not covered by RLS, and each needs its own control:** materialized views
cannot carry policies at all (banned); views need `security_invoker = true`;
`ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY` does not recurse to partitions; sequences leak
platform-wide counts (UUIDs instead, with a per-tenant counter row for the
consecutive invoice serials GST Rule 46 requires); pg-boss's own tables have no
`tenant_id` and no RLS, so payloads carry `{tenantId, entityId}` only and
`app_runtime` gets no privilege on the `pgboss` schema.

**Stated limitation, to be told to a reviewer plainly.** `app.*` placeholders
are user-settable, so anyone who can execute arbitrary SQL in the runtime
session can set another tenant's id. RLS here defends against *application
scoping bugs*; injection prevention is a **separate control**. These are
presented as three distinct controls — tenant isolation, intra-tenant
authorization, and injection prevention — because conflating them is the first
thing a competent reviewer pulls on.

### The connection-reuse test

"Runs through PgBouncer" is not the assertion. The failure mode is specific:
tenant A's request finishes, its connection returns to the pool, tenant B's
request is handed that same connection, and `app.tenant_id` is still A's.

**Corrected — the test as originally specified passes vacuously.** With a `pg`
pool of size 1, tenant A and tenant B ride the *same* PgBouncer client
connection, so PgBouncer never has to reuse a server connection across two
different clients. It would pass with PgBouncer absent from the path entirely,
and it would pass even with the exact bug it targets (`set_config(..., false)`),
because B's wrapper overwrites A's value before B queries. It cannot fail for
any of the reasons it exists.

What makes it real:

1. Testcontainers starts Postgres **and** PgBouncer; the test connects to
   PgBouncer with `default_pool_size = 1`.
2. **Two separate `pg.Client`s** — two PgBouncer client connections — asserting
   `SELECT pg_backend_pid()` returns the **same** value from both. That is the
   proof one server connection was shared, and it is the line to show a
   reviewer. Only a pooler can make that assertion true.
3. On client 2 after A's wrapped transaction: a wrapped B transaction sees only
   B; an unwrapped `current_setting` returns `''`, not A's id; an unwrapped
   select returns zero rows rather than a 500.
4. **A deliberately-broken variant** in which A uses `set_config(..., false)`
   and client 2 asserts it *does* see A's rows — documenting that the
   "no session-level SET" lint is load-bearing, and failing the day someone
   changes the pooler config without knowing why.

The unwrapped-query test must also run on a connection *after* a wrapped
transaction has ended on it, so it hits the empty-string state rather than the
fresh-connection state production is never in.

## D4 — One database, one schema per service

Shared tables with `tenant_id UUID NOT NULL` and RLS, per ADR-0002 — not
schema-per-tenant.

But **one Postgres schema per service** (`tenancy.*`, `identity.*`). It makes
"each service owns its tables" enforceable with `GRANT`s rather than convention,
keeps drizzle-kit migration histories separate per service, and still allows a
single transaction spanning services inside the composed host.

## D5 — The composition root

`docker/api.Dockerfile` already assumes `services/host/dist/index.js`, but no
such package exists and TOPOLOGY lists seven services.

The host must import all seven, which nothing else in the tree may do: `apps`
cannot (the `{ target: './apps', from: './services' }` zone), and no service may
import another. It lives at **`services/host`**, and `eslint.config.mjs` gains a
comment recording that `host` is deliberately absent from the `SERVICES` array
because it is the composition root. Today it is exempt by accident of what that
array happens to contain; it should be exempt on purpose.

## D6 — Identity, without a third party on the critical path

`services/identity` defines an identity **port** and ships two adapters:

- **WorkOS** — written and typed in M1, exercised when an account exists.
- **local dev** — mints principals from seeded synthetic users. Compose uses
  this one.

There is no WorkOS account as of M1, and typed-but-cold is the correct M1 state:
`docker compose up` from clean cannot depend on a third-party account, API keys
and network egress, and the isolation suite must stay hermetic.

## D7 — What of the connector contract lands in M1

Per the ADR-0015 correction, `tally-connector` does **not** import
`packages/contracts`; it keeps its own dated snapshot. So M1 publishes:

- **Contract types for our side**, matching the current `docs/KICKOFF.md`:
  `leaseSeconds`, the `X-Connector-Instance` header, dead-letter states, and
  `kind` modelled as **open** — known values as constants, wire type a string —
  so new kinds can be added without older connectors rejecting them.
- **`connector_keys` in `services/tenancy`** — hashed key, tenant, rotation and
  revocation state. It is tenancy-owned data, and because the key identifies the
  tenant it is a *second tenant-resolution path* alongside the IdP. Proving the
  tenant context works for both principal kinds is M1's job.

  **Amended 2026-09-03** after the connector-contract review. The table shape
  needs three things this originally missed, and getting them wrong means
  touching a two-reviewer migration path twice:

  - **Multiple simultaneously active keys per tenant.** Rotation without an
    overlap window is an outage per customer machine until someone visits the
    site. Admin mints key 2, the customer installs it, key 1 is then revoked or
    expires.
  - **`expiresAt`**, surfaced through `/health`, so a connector can warn before
    its key dies rather than after.
  - **A `connector_instances` registry** — first seen, last seen, version,
    hostname, disabled flag. This is how "disable one machine" works without
    per-machine keys, and it is what makes "the connector is down" a
    diagnosable support category. Tenancy-owned, alongside the keys.

  Keys are 256-bit random secrets, not passwords: SHA-256, not bcrypt. Store
  prefix plus hash, compare in constant time, show the plaintext once, and log
  only the prefix.
- **A machine-readable contract artifact** — JSON Schema emitted from the Zod
  schemas at build, which session B can diff its `CONTRACT.md` snapshot against.

Provisional values sent to session B, both marked changeable: **5 attempts**
before dead-lettering, **300 s** lease. One agreed semantic: on lease expiry
mid-batch the connector stops posting the remainder and re-polls rather than
finishing the batch. **Lease renewal is not an M1 concern** — it is M3.

Endpoints, lease bookkeeping, dead-letter transitions and staging tables: M3.

## D8 — Proving a policy cannot be dropped

Not per-table assertions — those pass happily when someone adds a *new* table
with no policy.

The suite introspects `pg_class` and `pg_policies` and asserts, for every table
in our schemas: RLS enabled, RLS **forced**, at least one policy present, and a
`tenant_id` column — against a short explicit allowlist for genuinely global
tables (`tenants` itself, migration bookkeeping). Adding an unprotected table
fails CI; so does dropping a policy.

The same introspection pass reads `information_schema.columns` and fails on any
`real`, `double precision` or `float` in our schemas. That is the "no float
anywhere in the schema" criterion, checked against the **live database** rather
than the migration text, so it catches a float introduced outside Drizzle.
